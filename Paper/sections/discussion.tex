\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Public-Data ESG Is Mostly Financial Quality}

The construct-validity result has a simple explanation. When direct
disclosure is missing, each proxy is chosen for a plausible link between a
financial ratio and an ESG outcome, such as margins and emissions
\cite{konar2001does}, revenue per employee and satisfaction
\cite{edmans_2011}, or cash returned and community investment. Each link is
defensible in isolation, but together they turn the E and S pillars into a
within-sector profitability and payout score. That score is largely spanned
by the financial factors, and it is what carries the index's forward risk
signal. The G pillar, built on third-party governance assessments, is the
only component that adds roughly orthogonal information, although that
information did not predict risk or returns in our window. Index builders
should therefore report provenance and redundancy per pillar alongside
coverage, and should not describe proxy E and S pillars as environmental or
social performance.

\subsection{Why In-Sample Evidence Misled}

The four validation pitfalls of Section~\ref{sec:res_insample} share one
feature: the validation target was not independent of what was being
validated. Trailing returns overlap the price history used by the
risk-adjusted and stability factors; the ``forward'' quality proxy shares
inputs with the factors, and the isotonic correction was fitted to it; and
the bootstrap perturbed scores rather than data, so it measured its own
noise. Each of these designs yields favourable numbers whether or not the
index works, which is the mechanism behind many replication failures in
factor research \cite{harvey_2016, bailey_2017}. The defence is procedural:
freeze the construction at a date and test it on outcomes that occur after
that date.

\subsection{ESG as a Risk Tilt, and Its Price}

The forward evidence supports a risk channel, but a narrow one. Higher ESG
predicts lower volatility beyond \NControls risk characteristics, including
trailing volatility, in line with \cite{albuquerque_2019}, but it does not
predict beta or drawdowns once sector and country are controlled. The
decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:res_mechanism} also limits what the channel
can be credited to, because the signal comes from the proxy-heavy S pillar
rather than from measured cells and is largely absorbed by recent realised
volatility. The defensible reading is that public-data ESG, as built here,
is a slow-moving low-risk characteristic assembled from financial data, not
evidence that environmental or social performance lowers risk.

The evidence also does not support a return channel over six months. In a
window in which both markets rose, the risk tilt was expensive, because an
ESG-only screen loaded on real estate, utilities and energy and held no
technology or healthcare firms. This is the trade-off that equilibrium models
predict, in which sustainable assets serve as hedges while offering lower
expected returns \cite{pastor_2021, pedersen_2021}. A single six-month window
cannot estimate a premium, but it does show the size of the regime risk that
an ESG-tilted mid-cap portfolio takes on relative to its universe.

\subsection{Implications for Index Builders}

Three practices follow from the evidence and cost little to adopt. First,
provenance per cell and redundancy per pillar should be standard disclosures
for any ESG index built from public data. Second, the construction should be
frozen and dated, with any number computed on the snapshot cross-section
treated as descriptive, and out-of-sample results should be reported with
their minimum detectable effect, since a few hundred firms can reject only
large effects. Third, before an out-of-sample effect is credited to ESG, it
should be re-estimated separately on measured, proxy and imputed cells and
controlled for recent risk. Generating the paper from the code also helps:
every discrepancy between manuscript and pipeline that our audit found was a
number copied or written by hand.
