\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section is organized by thematic streams that most directly shape the
methodological choices of this paper.

\subsection{ESG Rating Divergence and Data Quality}

A central result in the ESG literature is that commercial ratings disagree
substantially. Berg, K\"{o}lbel, and Rigobon \cite{berg_2022} show average
pairwise correlations near $r\approx0.54$ across major providers, with
divergence driven by scope, measurement, and weighting differences. Related
evidence finds weak convergence in firm-level assessments
\cite{chatterji_2016}, non-trivial portfolio consequences when provider choice
changes \cite{dimson_2020}, and return implications of disagreement itself
\cite{gibson_2021, avramov_2022}. Taken together, this strand frames ESG
ratings as informative but noisy, model-dependent signals rather than a single
``ground truth.''

Our approach differs at the data-construction level. Instead of treating
proprietary provider scores as primary inputs, we construct transparent,
indicator-level ESG proxies from public sources and track indicator provenance
throughout the pipeline. This does not eliminate measurement error, but it
makes assumptions auditable and allows sensitivity analysis to be performed on
the underlying inputs rather than only on black-box aggregate ratings.

\subsection{ESG and Financial Performance}

The ESG--performance literature reports mixed but generally non-negative
associations. The large meta-analysis of more than 2{,}200 studies by Friede,
Busch, and Bassen \cite{friede_2015} reports predominantly non-negative links.
Long-horizon evidence documents better outcomes among high-sustainability
firms \cite{eccles_2014}, and crisis-period evidence suggests an insurance-like
role for social capital \cite{lins_2017}. At the same time, economically
material issue selection is critical: Khan, Serafeim, and Yoon
\cite{khan_2016} show stronger effects for industry-material ESG issues than
for broad undifferentiated ESG baskets. Equilibrium models also imply that
``doing well'' and ``doing good'' need not map one-to-one in expected returns
\cite{pastor_2021}, while exclusion-based preferences can create pricing wedges
in controversial industries \cite{hong_2009}.

Our contribution in this stream is empirical design, not a claim that ESG is
universally return-enhancing. We explicitly test ESG--financial relationships
with proxy-based ESG data, report materiality-aware specifications, and make
construction circularity explicit as a first-order identification concern. In
other words, we evaluate whether ESG signals add incremental information under
transparent measurement assumptions.

\subsection{Multi-Factor Asset Pricing Models}

Classical factor models provide the baseline architecture for decomposing
returns: Fama--French three factors \cite{fama_french_1993}, Carhart momentum
\cite{carhart_1997}, the CAPM foundation \cite{sharpe_1964}, and the five-factor extension \cite{fama_french_2015},
with broad evidence for value and momentum effects across markets
\cite{asness_2013, jegadeesh_titman_1993}. This literature is primarily about
explaining cross-sectional expected returns with parsimonious tradable or
quasi-tradable factors.

Our framework builds on this intuition but has a different target. Rather than
estimating a pure pricing model, we use a composite scoring architecture that
combines ESG, financial, operational, market, and stability dimensions for
decision support. Thus, factor logic is retained, but the objective shifts from
asset-pricing parsimony to transparent multi-criteria ranking under explicit
investor preferences.

\subsection{ESG Index Construction}

Commercial ESG index methodologies typically rely on proprietary issuer
assessments, sector-relative normalization, and exclusion/tilt rules calibrated
to tracking-error constraints. Academic and practitioner work formalizes this
trade-off between ESG objectives and portfolio efficiency
\cite{giese_2019, pedersen_2021, drei_2019, alighanbari_2022, amenc_2020, msci_esg, sp_global, sustainalytics}.
The practical implication is that index outcomes depend not only on ESG
preferences but also on opaque data engineering and vendor-specific scoring
logic.

Our design diverges along three dimensions. First, inputs are reproducible from
public data with explicit transformation rules. Second, ESG pillar construction
uses SASB-style materiality weighting at the sector level. Third, we provide
three pre-specified investor profiles (ESG-first, balanced, financial-first),
so preference heterogeneity is modeled directly rather than embedded implicitly
in a single benchmark methodology.

\subsection{Composite Indicator Methodology}

Methodologically, this study is grounded in composite-indicator best practice.
The OECD handbook emphasizes transparent normalization, explicit weighting, and
robustness testing \cite{oecd_2008}, while multivariate analysis guidance
supports diagnostic checks for latent structure and redundancy \cite{hair_2019}.
In ESG contexts, these choices are consequential because indicator units,
distributions, and missingness patterns differ widely across pillars.

Our difference is implementation discipline: we follow OECD-style steps
explicitly, document each transformation, and validate weighting structure with
PCA-based diagnostics rather than treating chosen weights as purely normative.
This strengthens interpretability and clarifies where results are driven by
data versus by analyst preference.

\subsection{ESG Proxies and Alternative Data}

An adjacent literature shows that financially observable variables and
alternative data can recover aspects of ESG-relevant behavior. Examples include
environmental-performance valuation links \cite{konar2001does}, governance
quality proxies from entrenchment structures \cite{bebchuk2009what}, employee
satisfaction as an intangible signal \cite{edmans_2011}, machine-learning-based
ESG score prediction \cite{damato_2022}, and text/NLP extraction of
sustainability information \cite{sokolov_2021}. This stream is especially
important where direct disclosure is sparse or non-comparable.

Our contribution is to systematize this idea into a six-tier proxy pipeline
with per-indicator provenance tracking and explicit fallback rules. Compared
with one-off proxy studies, this provides an end-to-end framework that is
auditable, extensible, and suitable for reproducible index construction at
scale.

\subsection{Synthesis and Positioning}

Across these six streams, the literature motivates both the promise of ESG
integration and the limits of current practice: rating divergence, mixed
performance evidence, and opacity in index construction. This paper positions
itself as a transparent middle path---retaining multi-factor discipline while
making ESG measurement assumptions explicit, testable, and reproducible.
