\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sustainable-investment assets reached about \$30~trillion in the markets
covered by the most recent global review \cite{gsia_2022}. Yet the ESG ratings
that steer this capital disagree strongly across providers: the average
pairwise correlation is about $0.54$ \cite{berg_2022, chatterji_2016}. Ratings
are also proprietary, so their construction cannot be inspected
\cite{kotsantonis_2019, christensen_2022}. The gap is widest for mid-caps.
Large caps increasingly face mandatory sustainability reporting, while
mid-caps are covered by fewer analysts and data vendors, even though they are
large enough to be investable.

A natural response, and a growing practice in open-source quantitative
research, is to build ESG scores from public data. Such pipelines combine
regulatory filings, free governance scores and financial-statement ratios
with documented economic rationales, such as ``higher operating margin within
a sector implies lower emissions intensity''
\cite{konar2001does, edmans_2011, damato_2022}. These pipelines are
transparent, but transparency is not validity. Two questions are rarely asked
of them:
\begin{enumerate}
\item What does the resulting ``ESG'' score measure, once each input's actual
  source is traced?
\item Is the evidence usually offered for such indices valid, i.e.\ can it
  tell a good composite from a bad one?
\end{enumerate}

We study both questions on a concrete, fully reproducible system: a nine-factor
ESG--financial composite index for \NMid mid-cap equities in the US and India,
scored on a data snapshot taken at the close of \SnapshotDate. The index
combines SASB-weighted ESG pillars \cite{khan_2016, sasb_2023} with eight
financial factors and three investor profiles. It follows composite-indicator
practice \cite{oecd_2008}: robust normalisation, explicit weights and
provenance tracking. Every data cell carries a source label, and every number
in this paper is generated from pipeline outputs by a single script.

We then audited the pipeline end to end, as one would audit any published
index. The audit found implementation errors that changed the scores
materially. The most consequential were indicators whose direction was
flipped twice, which rewarded high emitters and high governance-risk firms.
It also found a family of \emph{validation} errors: designs that report
favourable numbers whether or not the index works. We fixed the
implementation errors and replaced the validation evidence with a genuine
out-of-sample (OOS) test. The universe, weights and three hypotheses were
fixed from the snapshot before any post-snapshot price was downloaded.

\textbf{Research questions.} Accordingly, we ask:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] (Construct validity) What does a public-data ESG score for
  mid-caps measure, and how much of it is financial information already present
  in the other factors?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] (Validation) How far do common in-sample validation designs
  overstate the quality of an ESG--financial composite?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] (Out of sample) Over the following \TradingDaysUS trading
  days, does the composite predict returns, and does its ESG component predict
  risk?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] (Mechanism) If ESG predicts risk, is that ESG
  information, a known risk factor, or past volatility carried forward?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An open, provenance-tracked index pipeline} for US and Indian
  mid-caps. It is reproducible from public sources, and its paper artifacts are
  generated from its outputs (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item \emph{A construct-validity audit of public-data ESG.} Only \NIndUsed of
  \NIndDeclared declared indicators carry information. The E and S pillars are
  mostly re-expressed financial ratios ($R^2$ on the other factors of \RSqE and
  \RSqS). Only governance, built from ISS scores (\NISSFirms firms) and ownership data,
  is close to orthogonal to them ($R^2=\RSqG$) (Section~\ref{sec:res_construct}).
\item \emph{A quantification of in-sample validation pitfalls.} These are
  trailing-return ``returns'', a contemporaneous ``forward'' proxy, a
  correction fitted to the validation target, and a misapplied bootstrap.
  They made a composite with no OOS predictive power look like a strong one
  (Section~\ref{sec:res_insample}).
\item \emph{A hold-out test with hypotheses fixed in advance}, Holm-corrected
  inference, bootstrap intervals and survivorship bounds. The composite does
  not predict returns, under the deployed or almost any other weights; the
  ESG component predicts lower risk (Sections~\ref{sec:res_oos}
  and~\ref{sec:res_null}).
\item \emph{A decomposition of the ESG risk signal} by factor, data source
  and horizon. The signal survives nine known risk characteristics and sector
  effects. But it lives in financial-proxy cells, not in measured ESG data,
  adds little to a volatility forecast, and fades given recent realised
  volatility (Section~\ref{sec:res_mechanism}).
\item \emph{A pre-registered second window}, with hashed scores, plan and
  code, which turns the next six months into a confirmatory test
  (Section~\ref{sec:prereg_design}).
\end{enumerate}

The results are negative on returns and qualified on risk. ESG as built here
does predict risk, as the view that ESG operates mainly through risk
suggests \cite{albuquerque_2019, lins_2017}. But the part of the score that
does so is financial information and slow-moving volatility, not
environmental or social measurement. The results also bear out warnings
about false discoveries and backtest overfitting in factor research
\cite{harvey_2016, bailey_2017}. Our practical message is simple. A
transparent composite can still be invalid. The evidence that tells the two
apart is out-of-sample, and even then it must be traced back to its data
sources before it is credited to ESG.
