\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sustainable-investment assets reached about \$30~trillion in the markets
covered by the most recent global review \cite{gsia_2022}, yet the
environmental, social and governance (ESG) ratings that steer this capital
disagree strongly across providers, with an average pairwise correlation of
about $0.54$ \cite{berg_2022, chatterji_2016}. The ratings are also
proprietary, so their construction cannot be inspected
\cite{kotsantonis_2019, christensen_2022}. The problem is most acute for
mid-caps. Large caps increasingly face mandatory sustainability reporting,
whereas mid-caps are followed by fewer analysts and data vendors even though
they are large enough to be investable.

A natural response, now common in open-source quantitative research, is to
build ESG scores from public data. Such pipelines combine regulatory filings,
free governance scores and financial-statement ratios, each justified by an
economic rationale such as ``higher operating margin within a sector implies
lower emissions intensity'' \cite{konar2001does, edmans_2011, damato_2022}.
They are transparent, but transparency does not establish validity. Two
questions are rarely asked of them: what the resulting score measures once
each input is traced to its actual source, and whether the evidence usually
offered for such indices can distinguish a good composite from a bad one.

We study both questions on a fully reproducible system: a nine-factor
ESG--financial index for \NMid mid-cap equities in the US and India, scored
on a data snapshot taken at the close of \SnapshotDate. The index weights its
ESG pillars by Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB) materiality
\cite{khan_2016, sasb_2023}, combines them with eight financial factors under
three investor profiles, and follows composite-indicator practice
\cite{oecd_2008}. Every data cell carries a source label, and every number in
the paper is generated from the pipeline outputs. An end-to-end audit of the
pipeline found implementation errors that changed the scores materially, most
importantly two indicators whose direction was flipped twice, and a family of
validation designs that report favourable numbers whether or not the index
works. We corrected the former and replaced the latter with an
out-of-sample (OOS) test whose universe, weights and hypotheses were fixed
before any post-snapshot price was downloaded.

The study answers four research questions. RQ1 asks what a public-data ESG
score for mid-caps measures, and how much of it is financial information
already contained in the other factors. RQ2 asks how far common in-sample
validation designs overstate the quality of such a composite. RQ3 asks
whether, over the following \TradingDaysUS trading days, the composite
predicts returns and its ESG component predicts risk. RQ4 asks whether any
risk signal reflects ESG information, a known risk characteristic, or past
volatility carried forward. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} maps the study design
onto these questions.

The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A construct-validity audit of public-data ESG.} Only \NIndUsed of
  \NIndDeclared declared indicators carry information, and the E and S
  pillars are largely re-expressed financial ratios. Governance, built from
  Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) scores and ownership data, is the
  only pillar close to orthogonal to the financial factors
  (Section~\ref{sec:res_construct}).
\item \emph{A quantification of in-sample validation pitfalls.} Trailing
  returns, a contemporaneous ``forward'' proxy, a correction fitted to the
  validation target and a misapplied bootstrap made a composite with no OOS
  predictive power look strong (Section~\ref{sec:res_insample}).
\item \emph{A hold-out test with hypotheses fixed in advance.} The composite
  does not predict returns under the deployed or almost any other weights,
  whereas its ESG component predicts lower volatility. Tracing that signal to
  its data sources shows that it comes from financial proxies and recent
  risk, not from measured ESG data (Sections~\ref{sec:res_oos}
  to~\ref{sec:res_null}).
\item \emph{An open, provenance-tracked pipeline with a pre-registered second
  window.} The scores, analysis plan and code for the next six months were
  hashed and published before that window opened, which makes it a
  confirmatory test (Section~\ref{sec:prereg_design}).
\end{enumerate}

The results are negative for returns and qualified for risk. The risk
finding agrees with the view that ESG operates mainly through risk
\cite{albuquerque_2019, lins_2017}, but in our data the part of the score
responsible is financial information and slow-moving volatility. The results
also bear out warnings about false discoveries and backtest overfitting in
factor research \cite{harvey_2016, bailey_2017}: a transparent composite can
still be invalid, and only out-of-sample evidence, traced back to its data
sources, separates the two.
